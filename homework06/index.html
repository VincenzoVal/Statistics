HMWK 6 — Contingency Tables and the Chi-Square Test of Independence

Marginal and conditional frequencies, expected counts, χ² statistic and p-value for a bivariate dataset (X, F)

1. Dataset and settings

A bivariate dataset is a list of observed pairs (x, f): X is the row variable and F is the column variable, and both are categorical. Three sources are available: a synthetic dataset in which F depends on X, a synthetic dataset in which X and F are independent by construction, and custom data pasted as text. The random numbers come from a small seeded generator (mulberry32), so a given seed always gives the same dataset.

Reading of the assignment. The assignment does not provide a dataset, so the page works on any pair of categorical variables and ships two synthetic examples. The synthetic data are generated by the code and are not real incident logs. Custom data are processed only in your browser and are never uploaded. Numeric variables must be grouped into classes before being used here.

One line per observation: x,f, or x,f,count for a repeated pair. Separators: comma, semicolon or tab. Lines starting with # are ignored. Each variable needs 2 to 12 distinct categories.

2. Contingency table and marginal frequencies

The contingency table counts, for every pair of categories (xi, fj), the observations nij. The marginal counts are the row totals ri = Σj nij and the column totals cj = Σi nij, and n is the total. Dividing by n gives the joint relative frequencies fij = nij/n and the marginal frequencies fi+ = ri/n and f+j = cj/n.

3. Conditional frequencies

Conditioning on a row fixes the category of X and looks at how the observations of that row are distributed over F; conditioning on a column does the opposite. The last row (or column) of each table is the corresponding marginal distribution, which is the reference for the comparison: if all conditional distributions are close to the marginal one, the data look compatible with independence.

f(F = fj | X = xi) = nij / ri      f(X = xi | F = fj) = nij / cj

4. Expected frequencies under independence

If X and F are independent, the probability of a cell is the product of the two marginal probabilities, which are estimated by fi+ and f+j. The expected count of a cell is therefore n fi+ f+j:

Eij = n · (ri/n) · (cj/n) = ri cj / n

The expected table has the same row and column totals as the observed table; only the way the counts are spread over the cells differs.

5. Chi-square statistic and p-value

Pearson’s statistic adds up the squared differences between observed and expected counts, each divided by the expected count. Under the null hypothesis of independence and for large enough expected counts it follows approximately a chi-square distribution with (R − 1)(C − 1) degrees of freedom. No continuity correction is applied, also for 2×2 tables.

χ² = Σi,j (nij − Eij)² / Eij      df = (R − 1)(C − 1)
p = P(χ²df ≥ χ²obs) = Q(df/2, χ²obs/2), with Q the regularized upper incomplete gamma function

The p-value (optional part of the assignment) is computed in the browser with a series expansion and a continued fraction for Q, without external libraries. The decision rule at level α is: reject independence if χ²obs exceeds the critical value, equivalently if p < α.

6. Independence in probability and in statistics

Probability: a property of a distribution

Two events A and B are independent if the probability of their intersection is the product of their probabilities. For P(B) > 0 this is equivalent to saying that knowing B does not change the probability of A.

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B)  ⇔  P(A | B) = P(A)

Two discrete random variables X and F are independent if the joint probability mass function is the product of the marginals for every pair of values, which means that the conditional distribution of F does not depend on the value of X.

P(X = xi, F = fj) = P(X = xi) P(F = fj) for all i, j  ⇔  P(F = fj | X = xi) = P(F = fj) for all i, j

Statistics: a hypothesis tested on a finite sample

In a sample the probabilities are unknown and only the frequencies are observed. Even when X and F are independent in the population, the observed frequencies fij are almost never exactly equal to fi+ f+j, because of sampling variation. Independence is therefore formulated as a hypothesis about the population and tested:

H0: πij = πi+ π+j for all i, j      H1: the equality fails for at least one cell

The unrestricted model has RC − 1 free cell probabilities; under H0 only (R − 1) + (C − 1) marginal probabilities are free. The difference, (R − 1)(C − 1), is the number of degrees of freedom of the test. The p-value is the probability, computed under H0, of a statistic at least as large as the observed one; a small p-value is evidence against H0. The test needs observations that are independent of each other and drawn from one population, and expected counts that are not too small for the chi-square approximation to hold (the page reports the smallest expected count and the number of cells with E < 5).

Discussion

Are X and F statistically independent or dependent?

The decision compares χ² with the critical value (equivalently p with α). The cells with the largest contributions show where the observed counts depart most from independence, and the conditional distributions of section 3 show the direction of the departure. Failing to reject H0 does not prove independence: it only means that the sample is compatible with it. Rejecting H0 says that the departure is unlikely to be due to sampling variation alone, but it says nothing about the size of the effect: with a large n even a very small departure becomes significant, which is why the page also reports Cramer’s V (extra), a normalised measure between 0 and 1.

With the synthetic datasets the true situation is known: the first one is dependent by construction, and the test is expected to reject it more reliably as n grows; the second one is independent by construction, and in repeated runs with different seeds the test still rejects in a fraction of runs close to α (type I error). The statistics change from seed to seed because of Monte Carlo noise.

Statistical association versus causality

The chi-square test detects an association between X and F: it is symmetric in the two variables and gives no direction. A significant result is compatible with X influencing F, with F influencing X, with a third variable influencing both (a confounder), with a biased way of selecting the observations, or with chance (type I error). In the first synthetic example, attack types and detection outcomes are associated, but the table alone cannot tell whether the attack type changes the chance of detection or whether the detection rules were simply written for some attack types and not for others.

An association can also change or even reverse when the data are split by a third variable (Simpson’s paradox), so a two-way table of aggregated data is not enough to support a causal claim. Causal conclusions need a study design that controls the other variables, such as a randomised experiment, or explicit causal assumptions about the data-generating process. Independence, instead, is the case where the two variables carry no information about each other in the population.

Key takeaways

  • Table and frequencies. The contingency table gives joint, marginal and conditional frequencies; independence means that all conditional distributions coincide with the marginal one.
  • Expected counts. Under independence Eij = ri cj / n, and the statistic χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E measures the total departure from them.
  • Decision. χ² is compared with a chi-square distribution with (R − 1)(C − 1) degrees of freedom; the p-value is the tail probability of the observed value under independence.
  • Limits of the test. Not rejecting H0 is not a proof of independence, and rejecting it says nothing about the size of the effect, which needs a measure such as Cramer’s V.
  • Association is not causation. The test shows that the variables are related, not why; confounders, selection and the direction of influence must be considered separately.
