HMWK 6 — Riemann vs Lebesgue Integration and the Numerical Normal CDF

Exercise 1: numerical integrals by partitioning the domain and by partitioning the range. Exercise 2: computing the standard normal CDF when its density has no elementary antiderivative.

Educational page. All computations use double-precision floating point and are not a numerical library. The page is public and uses no personal or real data.

Provenance: the functions, intervals and tasks come from the assignment. Every number in the tables and charts is computed in the browser by script.js, except the reference values in Table 2.2: the 5-decimal values are read from the NIST/SEMATECH table, and the 17-digit values were computed offline with the Python library mpmath at 40-digit precision. The derivations, the choice of methods and the interpretation are original analysis.

Interpretation notes.
  • A preimage measure cannot be computed in closed form for every f. The main Lebesgue-type method therefore estimates it on a fine grid of sample points; a random-point (Monte Carlo) version is shown for comparison, and exact preimage measures are used where they exist in closed form (f1–f6). The grid version is a reordering of a Riemann sum, so the two methods are close by construction.
  • Every double-precision number is a rational number, so the Dirichlet and Thomae functions cannot be evaluated meaningfully at sample points. They are treated through exact arguments and exact finite computations (Section 1.6).
  • Sizes N are odd, so that the jump and the kink at x = 0.5 fall inside a subinterval and not on a partition point. For f5 = 1/√x the integral is improper; the left sum is undefined and the midpoint and right sums are used.
  • For Φ the assignment points to a theory note. The numerical reference is the NIST table (5 decimals), complemented by high-precision values for a comparison to full double precision.

Only the Monte Carlo parts depend on the seed; their values change by about the noise shown in the tables when the seed changes. Everything else is deterministic.

Exercise 1 — Riemann and Lebesgue integration

1.1 Test functions

Seven functions come from the assignment (f1–f7); the Dirichlet and Thomae functions are treated in 1.6. For all of f1–f7 the integral is known exactly, so every error below is measured against a true value. For f7 the value is the sine integral Si(π).

1.2 Riemann-type sums (partition of the domain)

Split [a, b] into n subintervals of length h = (b − a)/n. The left, right and midpoint sums are Σ f(ξi)·h with ξi the left end, right end or midpoint. The lower and upper Darboux sums use the infimum and supremum of f on each subinterval; here they are approximated by the minimum and maximum of three samples per subinterval, which is not exact in general. 1.3 Lebesgue-type sums (partition of the range)

Split the range [m, M] of f into K equal levels yk = m + kΔ and let Ek = f−1([yk, yk+1)). The lower and upper Lebesgue sums are Σ yk μ(Ek) and Σ yk+1 μ(Ek); they are integrals of simple functions below and above f. Here K = N.

  • Grid: μ(Ek) is estimated as (b − a)·(number of the N midpoints with f in Ek)/N, and [m, M] is the range of the sampled values.
  • Exact preimages (no sampling): f1: μ(Ek) = 2(arcsin yk+1 − arcsin yk); f2: √yk+1 − √yk; f4: 2(yk+1 − yk) on the range [0, 1/2]; f3 and f6: two level sets of measure 1/2; f5: yk−2 − yk+1−2 on levels from 1 to √K, plus a tail bin [√K, ∞) of measure 1/K (no upper sum exists for this truncation); f7: no closed form, because the inverse of sin(x)/x is not elementary.

Same N as in 1.2. The bounds on the grid bracket the sampled sum, not exactly the integral.

The measure of each preimage is estimated by the fraction of N uniform random points that fall in it. The expected noise is σ(b − a)/√N, with σ the sample standard deviation of f; the actual error is typically of the same order.

Discussion

Accuracy and convergence. For smooth functions the midpoint rule is the most accurate at equal N, with error decreasing about like N−2. The Lebesgue grid sum adds a quantization error from the finite number of levels and decreases more slowly. The lower sum with exact preimages is a one-sided bound whose error decreases about like K−1, because each point is rounded down to a level. For the step functions f3 and f6 the Riemann error decreases only like N−1, since one subinterval always straddles the jump. With exact preimages the Lebesgue sum is exact with two level sets, whatever the position of the jump: its cost depends on the number of values taken by f, not on how irregular f is in x. For f5 all methods converge slowly, at a rate close to N−1/2, because of the unbounded singularity.

Efficiency. The midpoint sum needs N evaluations and no storage. The grid version needs the same evaluations plus an array of values and a histogram pass. The exact-preimage version needs no evaluation of f, but only works when the preimages are known analytically, which is rare. The Monte Carlo version converges like N−1/2 regardless of smoothness, which is far slower than the deterministic sums on these one-dimensional problems; its advantage appears in high dimension, which is not tested here.

1.5 Discussion: do the two approaches converge to the same value?

For a Riemann-integrable function the Lebesgue integral exists and equals the Riemann integral. This is a theorem, and the tables are consistent with it: for f1–f4, f6 and f7 the Riemann and Lebesgue sums approach the same exact value as N grows, the Monte Carlo estimates agree with it to within a few times their noise, and the column |R − L grid| in 1.4 is of the size of the larger of the two discretization errors. What differs is the path to the limit: the Riemann sums are indexed by the fineness of a partition of the domain, the Lebesgue sums by the fineness of a partition of the range, so their errors have different sources (variation of f within a subinterval for Riemann, level spacing and preimage measure for Lebesgue). For f5 the Riemann integral exists only as an improper integral, the limit of integrals over [ε, 1]; the Lebesgue integral of this non-negative function is the supremum over simple functions below it. Both equal 2, and the sums above approach it slowly from below or around it.

1.6 Functions on which the approaches behave differently

Dirichlet function: 1 on the rationals, 0 on the irrationals, on [0, 1]

Every subinterval of every partition contains both rationals and irrationals, because both sets are dense. The supremum is then 1 and the infimum is 0 on every subinterval, so the upper sum is 1 and the lower sum is 0 for every partition: the Riemann integral does not exist. For the Lebesgue integral the level set {f = 1} is the set of rationals in [0, 1], which is countable and has measure 0, so the integral is 1·0 + 0·1 = 0.

The values are computed from the rule above, not by evaluating f. A program cannot evaluate f at sample points: of 1000 pseudo-random draws, are dyadic rationals (multiples of 2−32), and every double-precision number is a rational number. Riemann sums with machine sample points therefore see f ≡ 1 and give 1, an artifact of the sample points and not the value of any integral.

Thomae function: 1/q at x = p/q in lowest terms, 0 at irrationals

Thomae's function is Riemann integrable with integral 0, because it is bounded and continuous except on the rationals, a set of measure 0. The lower sums are 0 since every subinterval contains irrationals. The upper sum for n equal subintervals is exact: the supremum on [i/n, (i+1)/n] is 1/q, with q the smallest denominator of a fraction in that interval. The Lebesgue view explains the limit: each level set {f ≥ 1/k} is finite, so it has measure 0, and the integral of a non-negative function with such level sets is 0.

The number of points in {f ≥ 1/k} is 1 + φ(1) + … + φ(k), with φ Euler's totient; the extra point accounts for both 0 and 1 being counted by q = 1. The upper sums decrease slowly towards 0 as n grows, while the Lebesgue computation gives 0 at once.

Other cases already visible above

The step functions f3 and f6 show the Riemann error decaying like 1/N while the Lebesgue sum with exact preimages is exact; f5 shows a Riemann integral that needs a limit, handled by truncating the range in the Lebesgue sum.

Key takeaways — Exercise 1

  • On Riemann-integrable functions both definitions give the same value, and the numerical sums approach it.
  • On smooth functions the midpoint rule is more accurate and cheaper than any Lebesgue-type sum computed from samples.
  • The Lebesgue approach is most useful where the structure of f is simple in its values and not in x (step functions), and where the Riemann integral fails (Dirichlet) or needs a limit (1/√x).
  • Monte Carlo noise is of the order σ(b − a)/√N and does not depend on the smoothness of f.

Exercise 2 — Numerical computation of the normal CDF

2.1 Method

The standard normal CDF is Φ(x) = ∫−∞x φ(u) du with φ(u) = e−u²/2/√(2π). By symmetry and the substitution u = √2·s:

Φ(x) = ½ + (1/√(2π)) ∫0x e−u²/2 du = ½ + (1/√π) ∫0x/√2 e−s² ds = ½ [1 + erf(x/√2)] = ½ erfc(−x/√2)

so it suffices to compute erf and erfc, with t = |x|/√2. Two representations are used, each where it is accurate:

  • Series for t < 2. Integrating the Maclaurin series of e−s² gives an alternating series, which loses digits through cancellation when t is not small. A series with positive terms avoids this. Let y(t) = et²∫0t e−s² ds. Then y′ = 1 + 2ty, y(0) = 0. Writing y = Σ cnt2n+1 gives c0 = 1 and (2n+1)cn = 2cn−1, hence
    erf(t) = (2/√π) e−t² Σn≥0 2n t2n+1 / (1·3·5···(2n+1))
    All terms are positive and the series converges for every t.
  • Continued fraction for t ≥ 2. In the tails 1 − Φ is tiny, and computing it as 1 − erf loses all relative accuracy. The continued fraction in DLMF 7.9.2,
    √π et² erfc(t) = 2t / (2t²+1 − (1·2)/(2t²+5 − (3·4)/(2t²+9 − ···)))
    converges quickly for t ≥ 2 and is evaluated with the modified Lentz method, stopping when a correction is below 10−16.
  • Negative arguments: Φ(−x) is the upper tail ½ erfc(x/√2), computed directly, not as 1 − Φ(x).

2.2 Comparison with a trusted reference

The NIST/SEMATECH table lists the area between 0 and x to 5 decimals; Φ(x) is that area plus 0.5, and for negative x NIST uses Φ(−x) = 1 − Φ(x). Rows without a NIST value are tail points; there the comparison uses the 17-digit reference computed offline with mpmath.

Discussion

The series with positive terms reaches full double precision with a number of terms that grows with t, because the terms first increase before decreasing; the continued fraction needs fewer iterations as t grows, which is why the two representations are complementary. Both cost a few dozen floating-point operations per evaluation, far less than quadrature, which needs one density evaluation per subinterval. Trapezoid and Simpson errors decrease algebraically with n, then stop at the roundoff level.

2.4 Discussion: why Φ is not an elementary function (Liouville)

An elementary function is one built from rational functions, exponentials and logarithms (hence also roots, trigonometric functions and their inverses) by finitely many sums, products, quotients and compositions. Φ is the antiderivative of φ that vanishes at −∞, so the question is whether e−x²/2 has an elementary antiderivative.

Theorem (Liouville). Let f and g be rational functions with g non-constant. Then f·eg has an elementary antiderivative if and only if there is a rational function r with

r′ + g′ r = f, and then ∫ f eg dx = r eg + C.

Application. Take f = 1 and g = −x²/2, so g′ = −x. An elementary antiderivative exists only if some rational r satisfies r′ − x r = 1. None does:

  1. Suppose r has a pole of order m ≥ 1 at some point x0. Then r′ has a pole of order m + 1 there, while x r has a pole of order at most m. So r′ − x r has a pole of order m + 1 ≥ 2 at x0 and cannot equal 1.
  2. Hence r is a polynomial. If r = 0, then r′ − x r = 0 ≠ 1. If deg r = d ≥ 0, then deg(x r) = d + 1 and deg r′ ≤ d − 1, so deg(r′ − x r) = d + 1 ≥ 1, and the polynomial r′ − x r cannot be the constant 1.

So e−x²/2 has no elementary antiderivative, and Φ is not an elementary function. The proof of the theorem itself is not reproduced here; the argument above is complete once the theorem is accepted. Note that the definite integral over the whole line is elementary: ∫−∞∞ φ = 1, obtained by a two-dimensional trick in polar coordinates, not by an antiderivative. The non-existence concerns the antiderivative as a function of x.

2.5 Discussion: erf as an exact representation, and why numerical methods remain necessary

The error function is defined by erf(t) = (2/√π)∫0t e−s² ds. This adds a new named function to the repertoire, and the formula in 2.1 then expresses Φ exactly: Φ(x) = ½[1 + erf(x/√2)]. It is an exact analytical representation, but not a closed form in elementary functions, which is what Liouville's theorem excludes. Naming a function does not compute it. A numerical value still requires an algorithm, and each natural choice has a limitation: the defining integral needs quadrature; the Maclaurin series converges for every t but, in its alternating form, loses accuracy through cancellation as t grows (shown in 2.6); the asymptotic series and the continued fraction are accurate in the tails and poor near zero; and fixed rational approximations such as A&S 7.1.26 are fast but limited to a few digits. A practical implementation therefore combines representations, as in 2.1.

Discussion

Quadrature is the simplest to write and needs no special function, but its cost grows with the accuracy required. The alternating Maclaurin series is accurate for small x and breaks down for large x, since the terms grow to a large size before cancelling; the positive-term form removes this. The A&S approximation is the cheapest and is accurate only to about 10−7. In the upper tail the relative error of the subtraction 1 − Φ(x) grows rapidly with x, because an absolute error of about 10−16 is divided by an ever smaller Q(x), while the continued fraction keeps a relative error close to roundoff.

Key takeaways — Exercise 2

  • Φ(x) = ½[1 + erf(x/√2)] is exact but not elementary: Liouville's theorem rules out an elementary antiderivative of e−x²/2.
  • Accurate evaluation uses different representations in different regions: a positive-term series near the centre, a continued fraction in the tails, and symmetry for negative x.
  • The implementation agrees with the NIST table to its 5 decimals and with a high-precision reference to about 10−14 relative error or better in these tests.
  • In the upper tail, compute Q(x) directly; 1 − Φ(x) cancels.
