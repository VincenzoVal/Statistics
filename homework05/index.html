Exercise 1 — Riemann and Lebesgue Integration

Note: all the sizes N used are odd, so that the jump and the kink at x = 0.5 fall inside a subinterval and not on a partition point. With an even N the midpoint sums of f3 and f6 would be exact and would hide the effect of the jump.

1.3 Riemann-type sums (partition of the domain)

Split [a, b] into n subintervals of length h = (b − a)/n. The left, right and midpoint sums are Σ f(ξi)·h, with ξi the left end, the right end or the midpoint of each subinterval. The lower and upper Darboux sums use the infimum and the supremum of f on each subinterval; here they are approximated by the minimum and the maximum of three samples per subinterval, which is not exact in general. 1.4 Lebesgue-type sums (partition of the range)

Split the range [m, M] of f into K equal levels yk = m + kΔ and let Ek = f−1([yk, yk+1)). The lower and upper Lebesgue sums are Σ yk μ(Ek) and Σ yk+1 μ(Ek): they are integrals of simple functions below and above f. Here K = N. The two sums differ by Δ(b − a), where Δ = (M − m)/K is the level spacing, because the sets Ek partition [a, b]: the integral lies between them and the error of either sum is at most Δ(b − a), of order 1/K. (For f5 the tail bin is unbounded, so no upper sum exists.)

  • Grid. μ(Ek) is estimated as (b − a) times the number of the N midpoints with f in Ek, divided by N, and [m, M] is the range of the sampled values.
  • Exact preimages (no sampling).
    • f1: μ(Ek) = 2(arcsin yk+1 − arcsin yk).
    • f2: √yk+1 − √yk.
    • f4: 2(yk+1 − yk) on the range [0, 1/2].
    • f3 and f6: two level sets of measure 1/2.
    • f5: yk−2 − yk+1−2 on levels from 1 to √K, plus a tail bin [√K, ∞) of measure 1/K (no upper sum exists for this truncation).
    • f7: no closed form, because the inverse of sin(x)/x is not elementary.

Same N as in section 1.3. The grid bounds bracket the sampled sum, not exactly the integral.

Note: a preimage measure cannot be computed in closed form for every f. The main Lebesgue-type method therefore estimates it on a fine grid of sample points; a random-point (Monte Carlo) version is compared with it in section 1.5, and exact preimage measures are used where they exist in closed form (f1–f6). The grid version is a reordering of the midpoint Riemann sum, up to the quantization of the values, so the two methods are close by construction.

1.6 Functions on which the approaches behave differently

Dirichlet function: 1 on the rationals, 0 on the irrationals, on [0, 1]

Every subinterval of every partition contains both rationals and irrationals, because both sets are dense. The supremum is then 1 and the infimum is 0 on every subinterval, so the upper sum is 1 and the lower sum is 0 for every partition: the Riemann integral does not exist. For the Lebesgue integral the level set {f = 1} is the set of rationals in [0, 1], which is countable and has measure 0, so the integral is 1·0 + 0·1 = 0.

Note: the values are computed from the rule above, not by evaluating f. A program cannot evaluate f at sample points: every double-precision number is a rational number, and of 1000 pseudo-random draws are dyadic rationals (multiples of 2−32). Riemann sums with machine sample points therefore see f ≡ 1 and give 1, which is an artifact of the sample points and not the value of any integral.

Thomae function: 1/q at x = p/q in lowest terms, 0 at the irrationals

Thomae’s function is Riemann integrable with integral 0, because it is bounded and continuous except on the rationals, a set of measure 0. The lower sums are 0 since every subinterval contains irrationals. The upper sum for n equal subintervals is exact: the supremum on [i/n, (i+1)/n] is 1/q, with q the smallest denominator of a fraction in that interval. The Lebesgue view explains the limit: each level set {f ≥ 1/k} is finite, so it has measure 0, and the integral of a non-negative function with such level sets is 0.

The number of points in {f ≥ 1/k} is 1 + φ(1) + … + φ(k), with φ Euler’s totient; the extra point accounts for both 0 and 1 having q = 1. The upper sums decrease slowly towards 0 as n grows, while the Lebesgue computation gives 0 at once.

Note: for the same reason (every double-precision number is rational), Thomae’s function is not evaluated at sample points: its upper sums are computed exactly from the smallest denominator in each subinterval.

Exercise 2 — Numerical Computation of the Normal CDF

2.1 The method for the normal CDF

The standard normal CDF is Φ(x) = ∫−∞x φ(u) du with φ(u) = e−u²/2/√(2π). By symmetry and the substitution u = √2·s:

Φ(x) = ½ + (1/√(2π)) ∫0x e−u²/2 du = ½ + (1/√π) ∫0x/√2 e−s² ds = ½ [1 + erf(x/√2)] = ½ erfc(−x/√2)

So it is enough to compute erf and erfc, with t = |x|/√2. Two representations are used, each where it is accurate.

  • Series for t < 2. Integrating the Maclaurin series of e−s² gives an alternating series, which loses digits through cancellation when t is not small. A series with positive terms avoids this. Let y(t) = et² ∫0t e−s² ds. Then y′ = 1 + 2ty and y(0) = 0. Writing y = Σ cn t2n+1 gives c0 = 1 and (2n+1) cn = 2 cn−1, hence
    erf(t) = (2/√π) e−t² Σn≥0 2n t2n+1 / (1·3·5·…·(2n+1))
    All the terms are positive and the series converges for every t.
  • Continued fraction for t ≥ 2. In the tails 1 − Φ is tiny, and computing it as 1 − erf loses all relative accuracy. The continued fraction in DLMF 7.9.2,
    √π et² erfc(t) = 2t / (2t²+1 − (1·2)/(2t²+5 − (3·4)/(2t²+9 − …)))
    converges quickly for t ≥ 2. It is evaluated with the modified Lentz method and stopped when a correction is below 10−16.
  • Negative arguments. Φ(−x) is the upper tail ½ erfc(x/√2), computed directly and not as 1 − Φ(x).

Discussion

Exercise 1: accuracy, convergence and efficiency

  • Accuracy and convergence. For smooth functions the midpoint rule is the most accurate at equal N, with an error that decreases about like N−2. The Lebesgue grid sum adds a quantization error from the finite number of levels, and decreases more slowly. The lower sum with exact preimages is a one-sided bound whose error decreases about like K−1, because each point is rounded down to a level. For the step functions f3 and f6 the Riemann error decreases only like N−1, since one subinterval always straddles the jump. With exact preimages the Lebesgue sum is exact with two level sets, wherever the jump is: its cost depends on the number of values taken by f, not on how irregular f is in x. For f5 all methods converge slowly, at a rate close to N−1/2, because of the unbounded singularity.

  • Efficiency. The midpoint sum needs N evaluations and no storage. The grid version needs the same evaluations plus an array of values and a histogram pass. The exact-preimage version needs no evaluation of f, but it works only when the preimages are known analytically, which is rare. The Monte Carlo version converges like N−1/2 whatever the smoothness, which is far slower than the deterministic sums on these one-dimensional problems; its advantage appears in high dimension, which is not tested here.

Exercise 1: do the two approaches converge to the same value?

  • For a Riemann-integrable function the Lebesgue integral exists and equals the Riemann integral. This is a theorem, and the tables of section 1.5 are consistent with it: for f1–f4, f6 and f7 the Riemann and Lebesgue sums approach the same exact value as N grows, the Monte Carlo estimates agree with it to within a few times their noise, and the column |R − L grid| is of the size of the larger of the two discretization errors. What differs is the path to the limit. The Riemann sums are indexed by the fineness of a partition of the domain, the Lebesgue sums by the fineness of a partition of the range, so their errors have different sources: the variation of f within a subinterval for Riemann, the level spacing and the preimage measure for Lebesgue. For f5 the Riemann integral exists only as an improper integral, the limit of the integrals over [ε, 1]; the Lebesgue integral of this non-negative function is the supremum over the simple functions below it. Both equal 2, and the sums approach it slowly.

Exercise 1: where the approaches differ

  • The Dirichlet function has no Riemann integral and a Lebesgue integral equal to 0; the Thomae function has both, equal to 0, and its upper Darboux sums approach 0 slowly while the Lebesgue computation gives 0 at once (section 1.6). The step functions f3 and f6 show a Riemann error that decays like 1/N, while the Lebesgue sum with exact preimages is exact; f5 shows a Riemann integral that needs a limit, handled in the Lebesgue sum by truncating the range.

Exercise 2: accuracy and cost

  • The series with positive terms reaches full double precision with a number of terms that grows with t, because the terms first increase before decreasing; the continued fraction needs fewer iterations as t grows, which is why the two representations are complementary. Both cost a few dozen floating-point operations per evaluation, far less than quadrature, which needs one density evaluation per subinterval. The trapezoid and Simpson errors decrease algebraically with n and then stop at the roundoff level.

Exercise 2: why the normal CDF is not an elementary function (Liouville)

  • An elementary function is one built from rational functions, exponentials and logarithms (hence also roots, trigonometric functions and their inverses) by finitely many sums, products, quotients and compositions. Φ is the antiderivative of φ that vanishes at −∞, so the question is whether e−x²/2 has an elementary antiderivative.

  • Theorem (Liouville). Let f and g be rational functions, with g non-constant. Then f·eg has an elementary antiderivative if and only if there is a rational function r such that

    r′ + g′ r = f, and then ∫ f eg dx = r eg + C.

  • Application. Take f = 1 and g = −x²/2, so g′ = −x. An elementary antiderivative exists only if some rational r satisfies r′ − x r = 1. None does:

    1. Suppose r has a pole of order m ≥ 1 at some point x0. Then r′ has a pole of order m + 1 there, while x r has a pole of order at most m. So r′ − x r has a pole of order m + 1 ≥ 2 at x0 and cannot equal 1.
    2. Hence r is a polynomial. If r = 0, then r′ − x r = 0 ≠ 1. If deg r = d ≥ 0, then deg(x r) = d + 1 and deg r′ ≤ d − 1, so deg(r′ − x r) = d + 1 ≥ 1, and the polynomial r′ − x r cannot be the constant 1.

    So e−x²/2 has no elementary antiderivative, and Φ is not an elementary function. The proof of the theorem itself is not reproduced here. Note that the definite integral over the whole line is elementary: ∫−∞∞ φ = 1, obtained by a two-dimensional argument in polar coordinates and not by an antiderivative. The non-existence concerns the antiderivative as a function of x.

Exercise 2: erf as an exact representation, and why numerical methods remain necessary

  • The error function is defined by erf(t) = (2/√π) ∫0t e−s² ds. This adds a new named function to the repertoire, and the formula of section 2.1 then expresses Φ exactly: Φ(x) = ½[1 + erf(x/√2)]. It is an exact analytical representation, but not a closed form in elementary functions, which is what Liouville’s theorem excludes. Naming a function does not compute it. A numerical value still requires an algorithm, and each natural choice has a limitation: the defining integral needs quadrature; the Maclaurin series converges for every t but, in its alternating form, loses accuracy through cancellation as t grows (shown in section 2.4); the asymptotic series and the continued fraction are accurate in the tails and poor near zero; fixed rational approximations are fast but limited to a few digits. A practical implementation therefore combines representations, as in section 2.1.

Exercise 2: the three approaches compared

  • Quadrature is the simplest to write and needs no special function, but its cost grows with the accuracy required. The alternating Maclaurin series is accurate for small x and breaks down for large x, since its terms grow to a large size before cancelling; the positive-term form removes this. The Abramowitz–Stegun formula 7.1.26 is the cheapest, but its error is about 10−7 and cannot be reduced, since the coefficients are fixed. In the upper tail the relative error of the subtraction 1 − Φ(x) grows rapidly with x, because an absolute error of about 10−16 is divided by an ever smaller Q(x), while the continued fraction keeps a relative error close to roundoff.

Key takeaways

  • Same value. On Riemann-integrable functions both definitions give the same value, and the numerical sums approach it.
  • Smooth functions. The midpoint rule is more accurate and cheaper than any Lebesgue-type sum computed from samples.
  • Where Lebesgue helps. The Lebesgue approach is most useful where f is simple in its values and not in x (step functions), and where the Riemann integral fails (Dirichlet) or needs a limit (1/√x).
  • Monte Carlo. Its noise is of the order σ(b − a)/√N and does not depend on the smoothness of f.
  • Normal CDF. Φ(x) = ½[1 + erf(x/√2)] is exact but not elementary: Liouville’s theorem rules out an elementary antiderivative of e−x²/2.
  • Evaluation. Accurate evaluation uses different representations in different regions: a positive-term series near the centre, a continued fraction in the tails, and symmetry for negative x. In the upper tail Q(x) must be computed directly, because 1 − Φ(x) cancels.
  • Accuracy reached. The implementation agrees with the NIST table to its 5 decimals and with a high-precision reference to about 10−14 relative error or better in these tests.
