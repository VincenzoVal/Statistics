HMWK 4 — Welford’s Recurrences and the Sampling Mean and Variance of Penetration Scores

Exercise 1: online formulas for the mean, the sum of squares and the variance · Exercise 2: what m hackers’ sample statistics say about μ and σ²

Overview

Exercise 1 derives Welford’s recurrence formulas for the sum of squares (SS) and the variance, in the same way as Knuth’s recurrence for the mean, and turns them into a three-line algorithm that processes the data one value at a time. Exercise 2 uses that algorithm in a simulation: m hackers each attempt n systems and obtain a score from 0 to 10 for each system; for every hacker the sample mean and the sample variance are computed with Welford’s formulas, and their averages are compared with the theoretical μ and σ².

Exercise 1 — Welford’s recurrence formulas

1.1 Notation and Knuth’s recurrence for the mean

Let x1, x2, … be the observations. After k of them we define:

Mk = (1/k) ∑i=1..k xi    (mean)
SSk = ∑i=1..k (xi − Mk)²    (sum of squares of the deviations from the current mean)
sk² = SSk / (k − 1)    (sample variance, k ≥ 2)

Knuth’s recurrence. Separate the last term from the sum: k·Mk = ∑i=1..k−1 xi + xk = (k − 1)·Mk−1 + xk. Dividing by k and rearranging:

Mk = Mk−1 + (xk − Mk−1) / k

The new mean is the old mean plus a correction proportional to δk = xk − Mk−1, the deviation of the new value from the current mean. The same approach, writing the quantity at step k in terms of the quantity at step k − 1 and the new value, is now applied to SS.

1.2 Recurrence for the sum of squares SS

Step 1. Expand the square in the definition of SSk:

SSk = ∑ xi² − 2Mk∑ xi + k·Mk² = ∑i=1..k xi² − k·Mk²

Step 2. Take the difference between step k and step k − 1; the sums of squares differ only by the last term:

SSk − SSk−1 = xk² − k·Mk² + (k − 1)·Mk−1²

Step 3. Use the relation (k − 1)·Mk−1 = k·Mk − xk (from the mean recurrence) to rewrite the last term:

SSk − SSk−1 = xk² − k·Mk² + (k·Mk − xk)·Mk−1 = xk·(xk − Mk−1) − k·Mk·(Mk − Mk−1)

Step 4. Knuth’s recurrence says k·(Mk − Mk−1) = xk − Mk−1, so the second term is Mk·(xk − Mk−1), and:

SSk = SSk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)·(xk − Mk)

Equivalent form. From the mean recurrence xk − Mk = (1 − 1/k)·(xk − Mk−1), hence

SSk = SSk−1 + ((k − 1)/k)·δk²,   with δk = xk − Mk−1

Start. M0 = 0 and SS0 = 0, so for k = 1 we get M1 = x1 and SS1 = 0. Remark. Mk always lies between Mk−1 and xk, so the two factors have the same sign and every increment is ≥ 0: SS can only grow and never becomes negative.

1.3 Recurrence for the variance

Dividing the recurrence for SS by k − 1 gives the sample variance, and dividing by k the “population” variance vk = SSk/k:

sk² = ((k − 2)/(k − 1))·sk−1² + δk²/k    (k ≥ 2; for k = 2 the first term vanishes and s2² = δ2²/2)
vk = ((k − 1)/k)·vk−1 + ((k − 1)/k²)·δk²

In practice one keeps Mk and SSk and divides by k − 1 only when the variance is needed: this is simpler and avoids a division at every step.

1.4 The code

The code is in JavaScript. The core of the algorithm is the three highlighted lines, and the variable n counts the values read so far.

let n = 0, mean = 0, ss = 0;
for (const x of data) {
  n += 1;
  const d = x - mean;        // delta_k = x_k - M_(k-1)
  mean += d / n;             // M_k  = M_(k-1) + delta_k / k           (Knuth)
  ss   += d * (x - mean);    // SS_k = SS_(k-1) + (x_k - M_(k-1)) (x_k - M_k)  (Welford)
}
const variance = ss / (n - 1);   // sample variance (n >= 2)

The algorithm is online: it reads each value once, needs only three numbers of memory, and the mean and variance are available after every step.

1.6 Why Welford and not the raw-sums formula?

The variance can also be computed in one pass from the two running sums ∑x and ∑x², with variance = (∑x² − (∑x)²/n)/(n − 1) (the “raw-sums formula”). When the data are large compared with their spread, this formula subtracts two huge, almost equal numbers, and the cancellation destroys the result (it can even become negative). Welford adds only non-negative increments computed from small differences. The test below uses 1000 integer values of the form offset + (a random integer from 0 to 10), so that the exact variance can be computed with exact integer arithmetic (BigInt) and used as reference.

Exercise 2 — Sampling mean and variance of the scores of m hackers

2.1 Set-up

Each penetration attempt gets an integer score from 0 to 10 drawn from a fixed distribution with mean μ and variance σ². There are m hackers and each attempts n systems, so each hacker produces a trajectory of n scores. For each hacker Welford’s formulas give the sample mean Mn and the sample variance s² = SSn/(n − 1); the simulation also records SSn/n, the “uncorrected” variance, to show why the denominator n − 1 is used. The values for different n are read from the same trajectories (a trajectory of length 1000 passes through the states of length 2, 5, 10, …), which is exactly what an online algorithm allows.

2.2 Averages of the sample statistics against μ and σ²

For every n the table gives the average of the m sample means, the average of the m sample variances, and the z-score of each average, that is the distance from the theoretical value in units of its own Monte Carlo standard error (the standard deviation across hackers divided by √m). Values between about −3 and +3 mean that the average is statistically indistinguishable from the theory.

2.3 Variability of the sample mean and of the sample variance as n grows

The standard deviation across the m hackers measures how much a single hacker’s estimate fluctuates. For the sample mean theory gives σ/√n. For the sample variance it gives √[(μ4 − (n − 3)/(n − 1)·σ4)/n], where μ4 is the fourth central moment of the score distribution. On log–log axes both are straight lines of slope −1/2.

2.4 Conclusions

What do the comparisons show?

  • The sample mean is unbiased. The average of the sample means equals μ for every n, within Monte Carlo noise: E[Mn] = μ.
  • The sample variance with denominator n − 1 is unbiased. Its average equals σ² for every n: E[SSn/(n − 1)] = σ².
  • Dividing by n is not. The average of SSn/n is (n − 1)/n · σ²: too small by 50 % for n = 2, by 10 % for n = 10, almost correct for large n. The reason is that the deviations are measured from the sample mean, which is by construction closer to the data than the true μ is, so SSn loses one degree of freedom (Bessel’s correction).

Why is this useful in inferential statistics?

  • In practice μ and σ² are unknown and only one sample is available. The sample mean and variance are estimators; being unbiased means that they do not err systematically in one direction, and that averaging over many samples recovers the truth, which is what this experiment shows.
  • Each estimate is a random variable with its own sampling distribution. The m hackers are a way of seeing that distribution: the histograms of section 2.3 are the distributions of Mn and of s².
  • The spread of the sample mean, the standard error σ/√n, is estimated by s/√n and is the basis of confidence intervals and of t-tests; this needs a reliable s², hence the n − 1.
  • Welford’s recurrences make these estimates available online, with constant memory and good numerical stability, even for data streams that never end.

How does n affect the variability?

  • The standard deviation of the sample means decreases like σ/√n: to halve the error one needs four times as many systems per hacker. The bias does not change with n (it is zero); only the spread does.
  • The sample variance also becomes more concentrated, with a standard deviation that shrinks like 1/√n, but it is a noisier estimator than the mean: relative to its own target it fluctuates more, and how much more depends on the fourth moment of the scores (heavier tails, larger fluctuations). For normal data its variance would be 2σ4/(n − 1).
  • Both estimators are therefore consistent: they converge in probability to μ and σ² as n grows, which is the Law of Large Numbers of Homework 4 applied to the mean and to the variance.
  • The number of hackers m does not change the quality of one hacker’s estimate. It only sets how precisely the experiment itself measures the averages and standard deviations (error of order 1/√m).
