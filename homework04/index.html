HMWK 4 — Large Numbers and Normal Fluctuations of the Penetration Score when considering many Systems and different Attackers

Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem by simulation

1. The model

There are n systems and m attackers. Every attacker goes through the systems one after the other. When attacker j attacks system i, the outcome is a penetration score Xi(j). All scores are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), with mean μ and variance σ². The path of one attacker is the random walk Sk = X1 + … + Xk, so there are m independent sample trajectories, each of length n.

2. Run the simulation

Choose the model, the number of attackers m and the increasing system sizes n. The simulation generates m trajectories of length up to the largest n and records Sn at every chosen n, so the distributions for different n come from the same trajectories. The random numbers come from a small seeded generator (mulberry32), so a given seed always gives the same result.

3. Sample trajectories

The same 20 attackers are drawn four times: the total score, the total score centred on its mean (Sk − kμ, which removes the drift of the Bernoulli model and makes the spreading visible), the average score and the normalised score. The black line is the theoretical mean and the red dashed lines are the mean ± 2 standard deviations.

Reading the plots. The total score Sk drifts upward like kμ (Bernoulli) and fans out like √k around its mean; the second plot shows this fan alone. For Rademacher μ = 0, so the first two plots coincide. The average Sk/k squeezes towards μ: each single trajectory settles down, which is the Law of Large Numbers at work. The normalised score Sk/√k neither explodes nor collapses: it keeps fluctuating inside a band of constant width, around 0 for Rademacher and around μ√k (a rising centre) for Bernoulli.

4. Distributions of the three quantities

Each plot shows the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) of the m values, one curve per n, from light (small n) to dark (large n). All curves of a plot share the same axes, so the effect of n is visible directly.

Total score. The curves become flatter as n grows: the distribution spreads out, with standard deviation σ√n.

Average score. The curves become steeper and steeper around μ (dashed line): the distribution collapses onto a single value.

5. Law of Large Numbers: convergence in probability of Sn/n

Sn/n converges in probability to μ if, for every ε > 0, P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) → 0. The simulation estimates this probability as the fraction of attackers whose average is farther than ε from μ. It is compared with Chebyshev’s bound σ²/(nε²), which is rigorous but loose, and with the normal approximation 2(1 − Φ(ε√n/σ)), which comes from the Central Limit Theorem.

6. Variance under the different normalisations

The sample variance over the m attackers is compared with the theoretical value. On the log–log plot the variance of Sn is a line of slope +1, the variance of Sn/n a line of slope −1, and the variance of Sn/√n a horizontal line. This is why √n is the natural scale: it is the only normalisation that keeps the variance fixed and non-zero.

7. Central Limit Theorem: convergence in distribution to the standard normal

For the standardised score Zn the table compares the simulation with theory. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) distance is the largest vertical gap between the CDF of Zn and the standard normal CDF Φ. The “exact” column is computed from the exact binomial distribution, with no randomness. The simulated KS distance cannot be reliably smaller than the sampling noise of about 1.36/√m (95 % level). Skewness and excess kurtosis are both 0 for a normal distribution; for Zn theory gives (1 − 2p)/√(np(1 − p)) and (1 − 6p(1 − p))/(np(1 − p)), with p = 1/2 for the Rademacher model.

8. Interpretation: system size n and number of attackers m

The aggregate score of the first m′ attackers on n systems is T = Σj Sn(j), a sum of m′n independent scores. The table uses the n chosen in section 4 and the first m′ of the simulated attackers. “Typical best” is the median of the best attacker under the normal approximation, nμ + σ√n · Φ−1(0.51/m′).

How n and m act on the aggregate penetration score

  • System size n changes what a single attacker achieves. The total Sn has mean nμ and standard deviation σ√n. The fraction of the systems compromised, Sn/n, has standard deviation σ/√n: the larger the infrastructure, the more predictable the fraction. This is the Law of Large Numbers. In absolute terms, however, the fluctuations of the total grow with √n.
  • Number of attackers m scales the aggregate. The expected aggregate T grows linearly with m (mean m′nμ) and its standard deviation like √m′. The grand average T/(m′n) has standard deviation σ/√(m′n): for this quantity, n and m play exactly the same role.
  • But m does not change the distribution of one attacker. Each attacker still ends up with the same Sn. What more attackers change is the worst case: the best of m′ attackers has a score of about nμ + σ√n · √(2 ln m′), so it keeps growing with m′ (slowly, like √(ln m′)). In the symmetric Rademacher case, where the average attacker gains nothing, the best attacker still gains a positive score of order σ√n.
  • For the simulation itself, m controls precision, not the phenomenon: the Monte Carlo error of an estimated probability or of the KS distance shrinks like 1/√m, so m should be large enough that the effects being studied are larger than this noise.

9. Key takeaways and limits of the model

  • Large numbers. The average score Sn/n has variance σ²/n → 0 and converges to μ in probability. The simulation shows the same on individual trajectories.
  • Three normalisations, three behaviours. Sn spreads (variance nσ²), Sn/n collapses (variance σ²/n), Sn/√n keeps a constant variance σ² and is the one that shows a limit shape.
  • Normal limit. In the Rademacher case Sn/√n converges in distribution to N(0, 1). For Bernoulli the same limit holds only for the centred and scaled Zn; the plain Sn/√n drifts like p√n.
  • Speed of convergence. The distance to the normal shrinks like 1/√n (Berry–Esseen). It is slower for a skewed Bernoulli (p far from 1/2) and for small n, where the discreteness of the score is visible.
  • Security reading. With many systems the compromised fraction is predictable, but the absolute number of compromised systems fluctuates more. With many attackers the aggregate grows linearly and the best attacker does better and better.

Limits. Real systems are not independent (shared software, shared credentials), real attackers adapt and learn, and the penetration probability is not the same for every system. With dependence or heavy tails the √n scaling and the normal limit can fail, and the aggregate risk can be far larger than the i.i.d. model suggests.
